- [X] Local horizon per location
Right now every planner assumes the horizon is clear all around. Tonight's best, the night planner, the Messier marathon, the seasonal planner and the beginner path all use a flat lowest altitude (30°). In practice observers have trees, houses or a hill to the south.

What's new: on the location page, the observer enters the altitude of the obstruction every 10–15° of azimuth (a small editor drawn on a polar plot, or an imported Stellarium horizon.txt).
What it changes: these planners check an object against the horizon at its azimuth instead of a fixed altitude. The night planner's limit uses TwilightSky/MoonlightSky-style altitude checks per point, so a check per azimuth fits in there. The field views would draw the real horizon.
Of the features listed here, this would make the plans most accurate for the most observers.

- [X] Your own eye, learned from your observations
The contrast reserve uses a model observer, with fstOffset as the only personal setting. The suspect-observation check already compares every observation with its predicted contrast reserve.

What's new: for an observer with enough seen and not-seen deep-sky observations, fit their own detection threshold: the contrast reserve at which they see an object half the time. Show it on the profile as something like "you see objects down to a reserve of −0.15; the model assumes 0".
What it changes: optionally use it as a personal offset in Tonight's best, Next for you and What's new for me.
The data is already there, so this mostly needs a fitting service and a profile card.

- [X] Seen by how many, per aperture
Aperture records show the single smallest telescope that saw an object. Across 190,000 observations there is enough data to show the whole curve.

What's new: on the object page, a "seen by X% of observers with 4″, 6″, 8″, 12″…" chart. It would use the DrawingComparison::CLASSES aperture classes, count seen and not seen, and apply the plausibility filter of ApertureRecords.
Why: the model's prediction would sit next to what observers actually saw. It's a strong addition to the object page, and cheap, because it's cached aggregate work.

- [X] Trip planner: "What could I see from…?"
An observer going to La Palma, Namibia or a starparty abroad wants to know what's new for them there.

What's new: pick a place on a map (not one of their saved locations), dates and an instrument. The list holds the objects that never rise high enough at home, or that their telescope doesn't show from their usual dark sky, ranked by popularity. They can make it an observing list with times per night.
It would reuse EquipmentGain (unsaved location instead of an unsaved instrument), TonightsBest, NightPlanner and SeasonalVisibility.

- [X] Your earlier observations at the eyepiece
When the current object at the eyepiece is one the observer has seen before, show their last observation(s): date, instrument, description and drawing thumbnail. They can then compare while still at the telescope.

It's small, and the eyepiece page plus the offline cache already hold everything needed.

- [X] Weather of the night filled in for sessions and observations
What's new: for past nights, fetch temperature, humidity, wind and cloud cover from Open-Meteo's historical archive API (the same provider as the forecast). Prefill the empty weather field of a session, and show "conditions that night" on old observations.
Why: it closes a gap in thousands of old sessions without asking anyone to type anything.

- [X] Star-hop routes for any object
The beginner path already gives a hop() from the nearest bright star.

What's new: generalise it to a chain of stars that each fit in the observer's finder field, from a naked-eye star to the object. Draw the chain on the finder field view and show it at the eyepiece.
Who it helps: owners of Dobsonians without GoTo, who are a large part of the users.

- [X] Mars and Venus: /mars-venus (View menu "Mars and Venus tonight", linked from the object pages of Mars and Venus) draws both planets as the telescope shows them in a night, at the moment they are highest or a chosen time, in the orientations of the Jupiter and Saturn page. Mars with its phase, the pole at its real angle, the polar caps of the season and about twenty classical albedo features (Syrtis Major, Hellas, Mare Acidalium, Solis Lacus, …), with the diameter, magnitude, tilt, central meridian and season (Ls, with the dust storm season); the central meridian every hour of the night with the features on it; "When can I see…?" gives the coming moments a chosen feature crosses the central meridian while Mars is up; the next opposition and closest approach (19 and 20 February 2027, 13.8″). Venus with its phase, diameter, magnitude and elongation, and the next greatest elongations, dichotomy and conjunctions. A table of the coming 16 weeks gives both disks.

- [X] Plain-language NGC/IC descriptions: the object page writes the parts of the description in Dreyer's abbreviations ("vF,pL,iR,bM", also the observers' "13''-pB,vS at 220X") out under it, "In plain words", in the reader's language (App\Services\DreyerDescription, phrases in resources/lang/<lang>/dreyer.php): 2,547 of the 3,098 descriptions. The history of each object (discoverer, date, aperture from Steinicke's Historic NGC) is not done.

- [X] Cool-down and dew advice for the night: the forecast of the coming nights (home page, location page) and the clear night message say from when the dew heater is needed and dew forms (per hour underlined in the cloud bar, with temperature and dew point in its tooltip), and when to put the standard telescope outside so its mirror has cooled at the start of the clear, dark hours, with and without a fan behind the mirror ("Put the Dobson outside at 16:35 (with a fan behind the mirror at 18:10) to have it cooled at 18:53"), from the forecast temperatures and the mirror's estimated thickness (App\Services\Weather\CoolDownAdvice), and whether the mirror keeps up with the cooling air.

- [X] Observing lists shared within a club: the owner of a public list shares it with a club to edit together ("The members may add and remove objects" when sharing, or "Let the members edit it" on the club page later); the members then add and remove its objects (also as their active list, from the object pages and searches), import files and edit the notes and times of its objects, while the name, description, publicity, the times of the whole list and deleting stay with the owner. - [X] Drawing templates for planets at the eyepiece: for Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn the sketch pad of the At the eyepiece page draws the disk as the telescope shows it at that moment (turned and mirrored like N and W): the flattening, the terminator of the phase, the polar caps of Mars, Saturn's rings with the part behind the globe hidden, and marks of the central meridian beyond the poles. "Fill the disk" paints the lit disk (rings and caps in their own shades, the night side black) and the Darken brush draws belts and markings on it; "Outline of the planet" turns the outline off. The sketch's details give the diameter, phase, Ls of Mars and ring tilt of Saturn, and the top lines the central meridian (CM of Mars, CM I and CM II of Jupiter) at the moment the sketch was begun, like the ALPO blank forms.

The privacy policy and the Delete Account section point to it. An observer with 1,528 observations and 431 drawings: 11 s, 32 MB.

- [x] Stellarium export and import. The Export menu of an observing list has "Export Stellarium (.sol)": the single observing list file of Stellarium's Observing list dialog (JSON, version 2.1), which its Import button reads. Stellarium finds each object by its designation; deep-sky objects also carry their J2000 position, magnitude and constellation, so Stellarium can still show one it does not know, and planets, comets and asteroids only their name. "Import objects" reads Stellarium's .sol, .ol (all lists in it) and legacy bookmarks .json files, by the designation, else the English or translated name.
